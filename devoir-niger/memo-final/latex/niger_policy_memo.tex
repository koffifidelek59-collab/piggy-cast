\section{Summary}
Niger faces a sharp rise in dangerous heat. Under current global policies, days of dangerous heat would
increase from 31 a year today to 91 by 2100. If warming stays below 2\degC, they would stay near 42. Extreme
rainfall would also intensify much more under current policies. Severe drought will affect almost the whole
country by 2030 in all scenarios. Niger should therefore support rapid global emission cuts and seek
adaptation finance now.

\section{A. Situation}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Agriculture and livestock generate almost 40\,\% of GDP and over 80\,\% of jobs. Both depend on
        rainfall, and most of this work is done outdoors (\WBtwentyfour).
  \item Climate shocks are already severe. The 2005 and 2010 droughts caused national food crises. The 2024
        floods killed 339 people and affected more than 1.1 million (\AFP).
  \item Niger's share of global emissions is negligible. Its exposure therefore depends on the emissions of
        other countries.
\end{enumerate}

\section{B. Projected impacts}


\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,start=4]
  \item \textbf{Heat (Table 1, Figure 1).} Dangerous-heat days triple under current policies, from 31 to 91 a year by 2100. Below
        2\degC, they stabilise near 42. Above this heat-index threshold, sustained outdoor work exposes people to
        heat exhaustion and heat stroke. The increase is about twice as large in the populated south as in the
        north (+44 against +21 days at 2\degC{} compared with 1\degC{} of global warming). WASCAL researchers
        project heat discomfort for over half of West Africa's population at 1.5 to 2\degC{} (\Sylla).
  \item \textbf{Drought.} The indicator includes evaporation, so it shows how heat dries soils and pastures.
        About 96\,\% of the land is affected by 2030 in all scenarios. Limiting warming to 2\degC{} does not
        change this. Only a 1.5\degC{} pathway reduces the share, to 87\,\% by 2100. Millet yields are expected
        to fall in most scenarios (\Mohamed).
  \item \textbf{Rainfall.} Extreme rainfall rises by 43\,\% under current policies by 2100, against 17\,\% below
        2\degC. Even the low end of the current-policies range shows an increase. Flood risk therefore grows in
        Niamey and the Niger River valley, where people, infrastructure and irrigated rice are concentrated.
        Extreme Sahelian storms have already tripled since 1982 (\Taylor).
\end{enumerate}

\section{C. Assessment}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,start=7]
  \item Limiting warming below 2\degC{} avoids about 49 days of dangerous heat a year by 2100. It also cuts the
        rise in extreme rainfall from 43\,\% to 17\,\%. A 1.5\degC{} pathway keeps heat near today's level
        (32 days a year by 2100).
  \item The economic cost is high. Climate change could reduce Niger's annual GDP by 2.2\,\% in a wet scenario
        and by 11.9\,\% in a dry scenario by 2050. These estimates exclude migration and conflict
        (\WBtwentytwo).
  \item Drought increases in all scenarios. A dry year reduces harvests and pastures, raises grain prices and
        forces sales of livestock. This leads to malnutrition, debt and tension between farmers and herders.
        Adaptation is therefore needed now, whatever the outcome of the negotiations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{D. Recommendations}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item Support the 1.5\degC{} goal. Ask major emitters for national plans consistent with a rapid phase-out of
        fossil fuels.
  \item Link Niger's support for any agreement to more adaptation finance, with simple access for Least
        Developed Countries.
  \item Seek support from the Loss and Damage Fund for droughts and floods, in coalition with the African Group
        of Negotiators and the LDC Group.
  \item At national level, fund drought-tolerant millet and sorghum, water harvesting, early warning systems and
        the protection of herders' mobility.
\end{enumerate}

\section{E. Limits of the analysis}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,start=10]
  \item These figures are projections, not forecasts. The direction of change is clear for heat, but its size
        varies between models (59 to 134 days in 2100 under current policies).
  \item The heat index uses an estimate of humidity at the hottest hour. The drought indicator counts a single
        month of drought, so it saturates. The tool does not model millet or sorghum, so its crop indicators
        (maize, rice, soy) were not used.
  \item These are hazard projections only. Given Niger's high vulnerability, real impacts are likely to be
        larger.
\end{enumerate}
