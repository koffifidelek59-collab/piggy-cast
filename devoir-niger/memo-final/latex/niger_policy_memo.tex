\section{Key message}
Models indicate that dangerous heat in Niger is projected to rise in every scenario, but far less if warming is
limited: about 54 days a year by 2050 under Current policies against 45 under Below 2\degC{} (31 today), a gap
that widens to 91 against 42 days by 2100. Extreme rainfall follows the same pattern, whereas severe drought is
projected to affect almost all of the country by 2050 in all three scenarios. Niger therefore has a direct
interest both in faster global emission cuts and in adaptation finance now.

\textbf{Dangerous heat.} The indicator counts days on which the daily maximum heat index exceeds the ``danger''
threshold of 40\degC{} (\CIE). By 2050, models project about 54 such days a year under Current policies,
against 45 under Below 2\degC{} and 41 under Net Zero 2050; by 2100 the gap widens to 91 against 42 days. This
matters because agriculture employs over 80\,\% of the population (\WBtwentyfour), and farming and herding are
outdoor work.

\textbf{Severe drought.} This indicator accounts for evaporation as well as rainfall. About 84\,\% of the land
already experiences at least one month of severe drought a year; models project about 96\,\% by 2030 and 97 to
100\,\% by 2050 in all three scenarios, with largely overlapping ranges. On this indicator, limiting warming
makes little difference before mid-century, so rain-fed millet, sorghum and pastures are likely to face more
frequent water stress whatever the global outcome.

\textbf{Extreme rainfall.} The heaviest five-day rainfall of the year could intensify by about 24\,\% by 2050
under Current policies, against 18\,\% under Below 2\degC; by 2100 the projected change is +43\,\% against
+17\,\%, although ranges are wide. Heavier downpours would raise flood risk in Niamey and the Niger River valley,
where people, infrastructure and irrigated rice are concentrated.

\section{Implications for Niger's national interest}
Compared with Current policies, Below 2\degC{} would avoid about
9 days of dangerous heat a year by 2050 and about 49 by 2100, and would limit the intensification of extreme
rainfall to +17\,\% instead of +43\,\% by 2100. Of the three indicators, heat separates the scenarios most
clearly: by 2100, even the low end of the Current policies range (59 days) lies above the central Below 2\degC{}
value (42 days), whereas the rainfall ranges overlap. The World Bank estimates that climate change could reduce
Niger's annual GDP by 2.2\,\% in wetter scenarios and by 11.9\,\% in drier ones by 2050. It links extreme
weather to low agricultural production, high food prices and food insecurity, and notes growing competition
between farmers and pastoralists for scarce resources (\WBtwentytwo). Because drought is projected to spread in
every scenario, Niger should defend the 1.5\degC{} goal in the negotiations while seeking adaptation finance for
drought-tolerant crops, water harvesting and early warning.

\section{Uncertainty}
These are projections, not forecasts, with wide model ranges; this memo emphasises 2050 because results after
2060 are indicative. The heat index relies on estimated humidity, and the drought indicator saturates. Given
Niger's high vulnerability, actual impacts may exceed what these hazard indicators suggest.
