\vspace{4pt}
\begin{graybox}{Key message}
\textbf{By 2100, current global policies would roughly triple the number of days on which heat becomes dangerous
in Niger, from about 31 a year today to 91; holding warming below 2\degC{} would cap it at about 42.} With more
than 80\,\% of Nigeriens working outdoors in farming and herding, this is the largest loss that global
mitigation can still prevent. The heaviest multi-day rains would also intensify far more under current
policies (+43\,\%) than below 2\degC{} (+17\,\%). Severe drought, by contrast, reaches almost the whole country
by 2030 in every scenario. \textbf{Niger's national interest is therefore twofold: press for a rapid global
phase-out of emissions, and secure the finance to adapt now.}
\end{graybox}

\section{1. Why the stakes are unusually high for Niger}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An economy that works in the open air.} Agriculture and livestock generate almost 40\,\% of GDP
        and over 80\,\% of employment, and depend overwhelmingly on rainfall (\WBtwentyfour).
  \item \textbf{Shocks that are already frequent and deadly.} The 2005 and 2010 droughts triggered national
        food crises; the 2024 floods killed 339 people and affected more than 1.1 million (\AFP).
  \item \textbf{Exposure without responsibility.} Niger emits a negligible share of global greenhouse gases.
        The losses described below are driven by other countries' emissions and can only be limited by their cuts.
\end{itemize}

\section{3. What limiting warming would change for Niger}

\textbf{The gains are large and concentrated where people live.} Compared with current policies, a
below-2\degC{} world would spare Niger about 49 days of dangerous heat a year by 2100, nearly two months in
which outdoor work, livestock and health are at risk, and would cut the intensification of extreme rainfall
from +43\,\% to +17\,\%. A 1.5\degC{} pathway would go further, holding dangerous-heat days near today's level
(32 a year by 2100). The economic stakes are of the same order: climate change could reduce Niger's annual GDP
by 2.2\,\% in a wet, optimistic scenario and by 11.9\,\% in a dry, pessimistic one by 2050, before counting
migration or conflict (\WBtwentytwo).

\textbf{Drought makes adaptation urgent in every scenario.} Because severe drought months spread to almost the
whole country by 2030 regardless of emissions, adaptation cannot wait for the outcome of negotiations:
drought-tolerant millet and sorghum, water harvesting, early warning and the protection of herders' mobility
are needed now. The danger lies in a cascade rather than a single number: a hot, dry year $\rightarrow$ failed
harvests and pastures $\rightarrow$ higher grain prices and distress sales of livestock $\rightarrow$
malnutrition, household debt and farmer--herder tensions $\rightarrow$ emergency spending that crowds out
investment. Every tenth of a degree avoided makes such years rarer and the adaptation bill smaller.

\begin{graybox}{Recommended negotiating position}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Defend the 1.5\degC{} goal, the only pathway that keeps dangerous heat near today's level and reverses
        the spread of drought, and press major emitters for NDCs consistent with a rapid fossil-fuel phase-out.
  \item Link Niger's support for any agreement to scaled-up adaptation finance with simplified access for Least
        Developed Countries.
  \item Use the Loss and Damage Fund for droughts and floods, in coalition with the African Group of Negotiators
        and the LDC Group.
\end{enumerate}
\end{graybox}

\section{4. Confidence and limits of the evidence}
The direction of change is robust for heat, but its size is uncertain: under current policies, models span 59 to
134 dangerous-heat days in 2100. The heat index relies on an approximation of humidity during the hottest hour,
a limitation the tool acknowledges. The drought indicator counts a single month of severe drought, so it
saturates and barely separates the scenarios. For extreme rainfall, the model range includes no change under
low warming but not under current policies by 2100. The tool's crop indicators (maize, rice, soy) were
deliberately set aside because Niger's staples, millet and sorghum, are not modelled. Finally, these figures
are hazard projections, not forecasts: given Niger's high social vulnerability, real impacts are likely to be
larger.
