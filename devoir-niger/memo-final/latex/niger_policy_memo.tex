\section{Key message}
Models indicate that dangerous heat in Niger is projected to rise from about 31 days a year today to 54 by 2050
under Current policies, against 45 under Below 2\degC{} and 41 under Net Zero 2050, the scenario consistent with
the 1.5\degC{} goal. Extreme rainfall is also projected to intensify more under Current policies, but model ranges
overlap, while severe drought is projected to affect almost the whole country in all three scenarios. Niger
therefore has a direct interest both in faster global emission cuts and in adaptation finance now.

\textbf{Dangerous heat.} The indicator counts days on which the daily maximum heat index exceeds the ``danger''
threshold of 40\degC{} (\CIE). By 2050, models project about 54 such days a year under Current policies, against
45 under Below 2\degC{} and 41 under Net Zero 2050, close to the 2030 level of about 39 days. Results after 2060
are only indicative, but the gap keeps widening: about 91 days [59 to 134] against 42 [29 to 72] by 2100. This
matters because agriculture employs over 80\,\% of the population (\WBtwentyfour), and farming and herding are
outdoor work.

\textbf{Severe drought.} This indicator accounts for evaporation as well as rainfall. About 84\,\% of the land
already experiences at least one month of severe drought a year; models project about 96\,\% by 2030 and 97 to
100\,\% by 2050 in all three scenarios, with largely overlapping ranges. Rain-fed millet, sorghum and pastures
are therefore likely to face more frequent water stress whatever the global outcome.

\textbf{Extreme rainfall.} The heaviest five-day rainfall of the year could intensify by about 24\,\% by 2050
under Current policies, against 18\,\% under Below 2\degC{} and 16\,\% under Net Zero 2050. The ranges overlap
and their low ends are close to zero, so this difference is uncertain. Heavier downpours would raise flood risk
in Niamey and the Niger River valley, where people, infrastructure and irrigated rice are concentrated.

\section{Implications for Niger's national interest}
Of the three indicators, heat is the one on which global emission cuts make the clearest difference. By 2050,
Below 2\degC{} would avoid about 9 days of dangerous heat a year compared with Current policies, and Net Zero 2050
about 13; after 2050, Net Zero 2050 is the only scenario in which heat days fall back towards today's level
(about 32 by 2100, indicative). The World Bank estimates that climate change could reduce Niger's annual GDP by
2.2\,\% in wetter scenarios and by 11.9\,\% in drier ones by 2050. It links extreme weather to low agricultural
production, high food prices and food insecurity, and notes growing competition between farmers and
pastoralists for scarce resources (\WBtwentytwo). Niger should therefore defend the 1.5\degC{} goal in the
negotiations and, because drought is projected to spread in every scenario, seek adaptation finance for
drought-tolerant crops, water harvesting and early warning.

\section{Uncertainty}
These are projections, not forecasts, with wide model ranges; this memo relies mainly on 2050 because the tool
marks results after 2060 as indicative. The heat index relies on estimated humidity, and the drought indicator
saturates. Given Niger's high vulnerability, actual impacts may exceed what these hazard indicators suggest.
